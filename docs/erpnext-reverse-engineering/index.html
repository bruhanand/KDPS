The ERP Engineering Handbook

A field manual for building any custom ERP — the durable patterns behind ERPNext, abstracted so you can rebuild them on any stack.

What this is

This handbook is not a manual for using ERPNext. It is a teardown of how ERPNext is engineered, written for the person who has to build an ERP from scratch — on Django, on Rails, on Spring, on whatever stack the client demands. ERPNext is one of the few mature, open-source, double-entry, multi-company, multi-currency ERPs whose source you can actually read. We read it cover to cover, isolated the patterns that recur across every module, and re-expressed each one as a stack-agnostic blueprint you can implement yourself.

The thesis throughout is that a good ERP is small at the core and infinitely variable at the edge. Almost everything that looks like a feature — an invoice, a stock transfer, a discount slab, a GST rate, an approval chain, a balance sheet — turns out to be data interpreted by a handful of generic engines. Get those engines right and adding the next business document becomes a schema edit, not an engineering project. Get them wrong and you re-implement accounting in every screen.

Key idea The variety of an ERP is enormous, but it is not structural variety. A purchase receipt and a sales return look nothing alike to a user, yet they are the same machine: a submittable document that, on submit, posts balanced rows into an append-only ledger and rolls a fulfilment percentage back to its source. This handbook teaches you to see that machine everywhere.

ERPNext in one breath

ERPNext is a business application that runs on top of the Frappe framework. The distinction matters enough that this handbook flags it on every page, because most of the cleverness lives in Frappe, not in ERPNext — and when you build your own ERP you will be rebuilding the Frappe half as much as the ERPNext half.

The b-frappe / b-erpnext convention Throughout the handbook, Frappe core marks a capability that the framework gives you for free (and that you must therefore build in a greenfield project), while ERPNext marks something the business app authored on top. A third badge, universal, marks a principle true of any serious ERP, ERPNext or not. When you scope your own build, the Frappe-badged items are your framework backlog; the ERPNext-badged items are your domain backlog.

The single most important consequence: in ERPNext, defining a new entity is writing one schema file, not wiring six subsystems by hand. The table, CRUD UI, list/filter view, REST endpoint at /api/resource/<DocType>, ORM handle, per-role and per-field permissions, and audit columns (owner, creation, modified, docstatus, name) all fall out of the framework reading that one declaration. That leverage is the prize, and reproducing it — even partially — is the first decision you make on a custom ERP.

The mental model

Hold the whole system in your head as five horizontal bands. Data and control flow top to bottom: masters are the nouns you transact with; documents are the verbs that record events; those events post into the two ledgers, which are the only source of financial and physical truth; a control plane decides who may do what and when; and a read side turns the ledgers back into reports. Underneath all five bands runs the meta-model (Ch 01): every box above — master, document, ledger row, settings singleton, child line — is the same kind of thing, a DocType. And cutting vertically through the document and ledger bands is the controller inheritance chain that factors the shared posting and validation logic so no document re-implements it:

A concrete document (say Sales Invoice) picks the right leaf controller as its Python class and inherits the entire stack via super(). That is why a new document type is "mostly a small declarative spec plus a thin posting hook" — the cross-cutting machinery is inherited, not copied.

Applying to KDPS KDPS's own architecture already mirrors this: master data → documents → ledgers → controls → intelligence, with the rule "documents write ledgers, reports read them." The five bands here are that spine. Where KDPS adds SOR/consignment, two-GSTIN cross-state transfers, and Tally as the statutory book, those slot in as data and edge plugins over the same core — exactly the regional-overlay move ERPNext uses for India GST (Ch 07, Ch 10), not a fork of the engine.

Ten meta-principles

These are the cross-cutting laws that recur in every chapter. If you remember nothing else from the handbook, remember these — each is a decision you make once, early, that shapes everything downstream. The WHY matters more than the rule.

1 · Everything is a DocType Frappe core universal

WHY: if masters, transactions, ledgers, settings and child lines all share one metadata shape, then one generic engine gives all of them tables, APIs, forms, permissions and audit columns for free. Model metadata as first-class data and adding an entity is one schema file. See Ch 01.

2 · Documents post the ledgers; reports read them universal

WHY: no document ever mutates a balance, and no report ever stores one. Business logic decides which accounts to debit/credit; one posting service writes the rows; reports are pure aggregations. This single separation keeps the books from ever drifting. See Ch 03.

3 · Submitted is immutable — amend, don't edit universal

WHY: auditors need a permanent record of exactly what was posted. A submitted document is frozen forever; to "change" it you cancel (posting equal-and-opposite reversals) and amend (a new draft linked by amended_from). History is additive, never destructive. See Ch 02.

4 · Double-entry is non-negotiable universal

WHY: total debit == total credit per voucher, enforced in exactly one place every poster funnels through (with a tiny round-off tolerance), is what makes the ledger self-checking. Balances are always SUM(debit) − SUM(credit), never stored. See Ch 03.

5 · An ERP is sub-ledgers feeding one General Ledger universal

WHY: stock, AR, AP, payroll are each their own append-only sub-ledger, and each physical event atomically posts both its sub-ledger row and a balanced GL pair derived from it. That discipline is what keeps operations and the books in agreement. See Ch 04.

6 · Snapshot masters into transactions universal

WHY: copy the master's name, rate, accounts and tax onto the line at creation, resolved through a per-company defaults cascade. The master is the editable "current truth"; the transaction is the frozen "historical truth." So you can fix a typo next year without corrupting last year's books. See Ch 06.

7 · Variation is data, not code universal

WHY: SKUs (template × attribute × variant), tax rates (effective-dated templates), discounts (priority-resolved rules), approvals (workflow tables) are all rows. A GST slab change or a new size axis is a data edit, never a deploy. See Ch 07 and Ch 06.

8 · Extend without forking Frappe core

WHY: a customer can be deeply customized and the vendor can still upgrade them, only if all variation goes through one closed manifest of named hooks, plus custom fields / property setters as data, plus separate installable apps (India GST lives entirely outside core). See Ch 10.

9 · Access is a data matrix Frappe core universal

WHY: authorization is a (role × action × permlevel) table plus row-level User Permissions, enforced by one engine that AND/ORs the rules into every query's WHERE clause. A "read-only auditor" is just rows with the write/submit columns left off — never new branching code. See Ch 08.

10 · Statements are live queries, never stored universal

WHY: Balance Sheet, P&L, GL and AR/AP aging are recomputed from the immutable ledger on every run, rolled up a nested-set account tree. There is no stored "account balance" to drift. Caches, if any, are reconcilable optimizations with a raw-ledger fallback. See Ch 11.

The trap these principles guard against Every one of these laws exists to prevent the same failure: derived state that drifts from its source. A stored balance that disagrees with the ledger; an edited invoice that no longer matches what was posted; a permission check buried in a controller that the report query forgets. The cure is always the same shape — one source of truth, derived everywhere else, enforced in one place. When you are tempted to cache, denormalize, or special-case, ask first whether you are about to create a second copy of the truth.

Reading order vs. build order The chapter numbers are a reading order (concepts build on each other). The build order is different and lives in Chapter 12: you implement the meta-model and the ledgers before you have a single business document worth posting. Don't confuse "where it appears in the book" with "when you write the code."

How to use this to build a new ERP

The two synthesis chapters are where reading turns into doing. Chapter 12 is the playbook — the sequence and the day-one decisions. Chapter 13 is the catalog — the entity inventory you copy from and adapt. Use them together: the playbook tells you what to build next; the catalog tells you which entities and flags that step needs.

The path, in one sketch

1 · Build (or adopt) the meta-modelschema-as-data engine: table + UI + API + ORM + permissions + audit columns from one declaration — Ch 01
2 · Build the lifecycledocstatus state machine, controller hook chain, cancel-and-amend immutability — Ch 02
3 · Build the two ledgersGL (double-entry, append-only) + Stock (qty + valuation + repost engine); one posting service each — Ch 03, 04
4 · Add the first documentsone O2C and one P2P chain; thin per-document composers that decide which accounts to debit/credit — Ch 05
5 · Add masters & the policy enginesItem/Party with snapshot; tax + pricing as effective-dated data — Ch 06, 07
6 · Lay in the control planepermission matrix, workflow, hooks/custom-fields — built into the meta-model from day one, not bolted on — Ch 08, 09, 10
7 · Turn the ledger into answerslive financial statements, metadata reports, dashboards, print formats — Ch 11

The decisions you make on day one (and can't cheaply undo)

How much of the meta-model do I build?
Full schema-as-data engine (maximum leverage, large up-front cost) versus conventional models with a few generic mixins (faster start, less leverage per new entity). On Django this is the choice between a metadata-driven model layer and plain models.py with a permission mixin + an audit mixin. Ch 01 and Ch 12 frame the trade.
Append-only ledgers, from the first commit.
The GL and stock ledgers must be insert-only and immutable before you post anything real. Retrofitting immutability after you have mutable balances is the single most expensive mistake in ERP work. Build the freeze-date + role gate and the repost engine early (Ch 03, Ch 04).
Snapshot, don't reference.
Decide that transaction lines copy master values at creation, and add immutability guards on the master fields the ledger depends on (valuation method, stock-item flag). This is cheap to do early and ruinous to retrofit (Ch 06).
Variation as data, extension via one manifest.
Tax, pricing, approvals and country rules go in tables and plugins, not if/else. Permissions go in a queryable matrix an admin can render. Settle this before the first regional requirement lands, or you will fork (Ch 07, 08, 09, 10).
The shortest possible summary Build a tiny, generic core — a metadata engine, a lifecycle, and two append-only ledgers funnelled through one posting service each — and push all the business variety into data and thin per-document composers. Then the next invoice type, the next tax slab, the next approval chain, the next report is a row or a small spec, not a project. That is the entire lesson of ERPNext, and the entire purpose of this handbook.
Applying to KDPS For KDPS specifically: the meta-model and lifecycle map onto the React-PWA + Django + Postgres stack already chosen; the two ledgers carry GST-tagged GL rows and SKU-level (style × size × colour) stock; SOR/consignment becomes a commercial-model dimension on the item plus a posting rule that defers vendor liability until sale; Tally stays the statutory book that the GL syncs into. Walk the playbook one vertical slice at a time (Ch 12) — the catalog (Ch 13) is your borrow-from list, not a spec to copy whole.

Takeaways

  1. This is a teardown of how ERPNext is engineered, abstracted into stack-agnostic patterns you can rebuild anywhere — not a manual for using ERPNext.
  2. ERPNext is a business app on the Frappe framework; most leverage lives in Frappe's meta-model. The handbook flags Frappe core (framework backlog), ERPNext (domain backlog), and universal (true of any ERP) on every page.
  3. Hold the system as five bands — masters → documents → ledgers → control → read side — with a meta-model underneath and a controller inheritance chain cutting vertically through the document/ledger bands.
  4. Ten meta-principles recur everywhere; they all guard one failure mode: derived state drifting from its source. The cure is always one source of truth, derived everywhere, enforced in one place.
  5. Build order ≠ reading order. Start with the meta-model and the two append-only ledgers; immutability and snapshotting must be in from day one because they cannot be cheaply retrofitted.
  6. Use Chapter 12 (playbook) for the sequence and Chapter 13 (catalog) for the entity inventory when you sit down to build.
