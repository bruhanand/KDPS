Markdown & clearance AI for fashion — what's real, what's hype, what you'd build

An honest, cited picture of how retailers use AI/analytics to decide discounts on stock they own (clearance of aging / slow-moving inventory), and what is realistically buildable by one developer for a ~50-store multi-brand Indian apparel retailer. This is research, not a design — it informs the markdown-recommendation feature, where AI only suggests and a human authors the price.

Compiled 18 June 2026 · every claim carries a source (name + link) · flags mark PROVEN MOSTLY HYPE YOU'D BUILD IT INDIA-RELEVANT

The short version

1. The mature technology is classic ML / operations-research price optimization with a dashboard and a human approval step — not an autonomous "AI agent". Every serious tool does the same thing: forecast demand, estimate how much a price cut lifts sales (elasticity), then pick the discount that clears the stock by a deadline at the best margin, inside hard business rules. The "agentic AI" branding flooding the market in 2025–26 is, for pricing, mostly a chat layer on top of that same engine. Gartner found only ~130 of thousands of "agentic" vendors are genuinely agentic and predicts >40% of agentic projects get cancelled by 2027.

2. For clearance specifically, the signals that matter are few and simple: sell-through vs target, weeks-of-cover vs the time left in the season, and age — plus, for apparel, broken size runs. Depth is then sized by elasticity against a margin floor.

3. The output is a recommended discount (a single % chosen from an allowed ladder, not a free number), with expected sell-through and margin impact, a step-down plan over the clearance window, and a priority order of which items to act on first — handed to a human to accept / reject / tweak.

4. For a 50-store retailer just leaving per-store POS + Excel, real ML is out of reach for now — it needs 2–3 years of clean, size-level history you don't have, and feeding thin/dirty data to ML loses to a sensible rule. A simple, explainable, sell-through-triggered discount ladder with a margin floor is not a compromise — it's what even the ML vendors say to start with. The rule engine you build now stays permanent; ML later wraps it, it doesn't replace it.

The one sentence to carry into D7 Build a rules engine that turns a few signals (age, sell-through-vs-target, weeks-of-cover, broken sizes) into a recommended discount band + a plain-English reason, hard-capped by a margin floor from PT/invoice cost; a human approves the actual price. Use an LLM, if at all, only to explain the recommendation in plain Hindi/English — never to compute the number.

1 · Who sells markdown / clearance pricing — and the trap to avoid

The distinction that matters most Two very different things get sold under "AI pricing." Markdown OPTIMIZATION looks at your own aging stock (age, sell-through, cover, size health) and recommends how deep to cut and when. Competitive-price INTELLIGENCE scrapes competitors' prices and tells you where you stand — it does not decide clearance depth on your dead stock. For KDPS clearance, you want the first kind. EDITED and Intelligence Node are the second kind (the latter is ironically an Indian-founded company). Daydream is neither — it's a consumer shopping app, not a retailer tool.

Genuine markdown optimizers (own-inventory clearance)

Increff INDIA-RELEVANT most transparent method

Bengaluru-built retail SaaS (700+ brands). Its Markdown Optimization recommends "more accurate discount percentages" at store and SKU level. Decides depth from True ROS (rate of sale with promo/liquidation noise removed) + product age + sell-through% + size-set health + elasticity caps "to prevent wasted markdowns"; timing from ageing + automated triggers when ROS/sell-through cutoffs are breached. Runs on own data only — no competitor scraping. Named Indian-fashion customers incl. Aditya Birla Fashion & Retail; also Levi's, PUMA, Tommy Hilfiger, Pepe Jeans.

Why it matters to KDPS: the only Indian-built true markdown optimizer in the field, works at Style×Size×Color with size-set health (matches your SKU rule), and publishes its logic most clearly of anyone — a good reference model even if you don't buy it. Enterprise cloud service. Most important for clearance of aging stock — confirmed Umbrex states it directly: markdown optimization "identifies SKUs with low sell-through and high weeks-of-supply, then schedules earlier markdowns to exit inventory before it becomes end-of-season liability." Increff's "most critical signals for aging inventory" are weeks-of-cover, sell-through-vs-plan, age flags, and broken size curves. So the trigger trio is sell-through-vs-target + weeks-of-cover-vs-remaining-window + age, with broken size runs as a first-class fourth for apparel. Depth is then elasticity against the margin floor. Umbrex, Increff, Eightx
Two honesty flags on the signals (a) Elasticity at clearance is contested. One camp says late-season demand turns inelastic ("even at 50% off, sell-through is low" — Peak); another says it rises so you must cut deeper (42signals). The robust, agreed conclusion is "act early and shallow," because discount response decays over time and cuts are non-linear. Treat the direction as a tunable assumption, not a fact. (b) Specific numbers (25% opener, 3 steps, 30/50/70 at 60/75/90 days) are rules of thumb, not constants — configurable defaults.

Definitions for the build: Sell-through % = Units Sold ÷ Units Received × 100; apparel healthy range ~65–85% (Toolio). Weeks-of-cover = On-hand ÷ average weekly units sold (forward version adds on-order ÷ forecast weekly demand) (StyleMatrix).

3 · What the recommendation actually looks like

Across vendors, a markdown recommendation is not just "a number" — it's a small package. All six elements below are documented across multiple independent sources.

A single % — but chosen from an allowed band/ladder, not a free value

The system emits a specific recommended price/discount with effective dates, but it must land on a configured ladder (5%, 10%, 20%…) and respect psychological endings (₹999-style). The "band" is a guardrail input (e.g. opening markdown allowed 25–50%, step change 20–40%); the single % is the output snapped to it.

o9; Oracle rules; Umbrex

Expected sell-through lift + revenue/margin impact (the core trade-off)

Each recommendation projects expected units, gross-margin and sell-through; Solvoyo shows "revenue, unit sales, and gross margin for all scenarios." The clear-faster-vs-protect-margin trade-off is an explicit, chosen objective — usually framed as "maximize gross-margin dollars subject to hitting a target sell-through by the exit date."

Solvoyo; Umbrex; Peak

A step-down plan over the clearance window — not a one-shot price

The output is a ladder over time: e.g. "10% now, 20% in three weeks, 40% if sell-through lags." Apparel cadence is typically 2–3 steps: first 10–25% (mid-season), second 30–40%, final 50–60%+. The rule "you can always discount deeper, you can never put the price back up" → cap the opening cut. Cadence is also operationally constrained (e.g. no more than biweekly price changes in-store).

Eightx; Impact Analytics; Retalon

A priority order — which SKUs/styles/lots to act on first

A core output is a prioritized list of markdown actions with expected impact + rationale. Logic: act first on low-sell-through + high-weeks-of-cover items to exit before season-end; concentrate discounts where price is the constraint, and "discount by style, never sitewide" — don't mark down the winners.

Umbrex; Eightx

Also documented: a markdown budget (a shared cap the optimizer allocates across SKUs) and the number of markdowns as a bounded decision (cadences specify "at least three price movements" before final liquidation). J. Revenue & Pricing Mgmt; o9

4 · Demand forecasting for apparel — the layer under markdowns

You can't say "this is underperforming, mark it down" without a view of what it should have sold. That view is a demand forecast — and fashion makes it genuinely hard.

Cold start — new styles with no history ("like styles")

Most fashion product is new every season, so there's no time-series to extrapolate. The standard method is forecast by analogy: borrow the demand curve of past styles that resemble the new one, matched on attributes (category, price band, colour, fabric, silhouette, season). Vendors implement this as attribute clustering: group existing SKUs by attributes, find the new SKU's cluster, borrow comparable items' demand shape (ramp/peak/decline) weighted by similarity, while the volume comes from buy quantity, price and store reach — then blend in real sales as they arrive, reducing the comparables' weight over time.

Lokad puts the problem bluntly: "fashion is about products that haven't been sold at all," and warns that manual old→new style mapping fails because each collection "redefines the codes" — the matching must be algorithmic.

Source: Forecast by analogy; onepint.ai; Lokad; academic: Singh et al., arXiv 1907.01960

Repeat / carry-over styles

The minority (basics, replenishment) but the tractable case: classical time-series with seasonality and ML on the item's own demand history. The architectural split that matters: history-rich SKUs → time-series/ML; history-poor (new) SKUs → attribute-borrowed forecast. Most fashion ranges are dominated by the second.

Size / colour-level forecasting (your SKU = Style×Size×Color rule)

A style-level forecast is useless for buying or markdowns — you split it to SKU with size curves: a distribution of demand across sizes that sums to 100%; forecast total style demand, then apply the curve. Curves are built from history and vary by store/region. Two hard truths: (a) broken size runs (popular sizes sell out, odd sizes become markdown fodder, and the lost full-price sales are invisible in standard reports) — IHL/Style Arcade size the global cost at ~$1 trillion/year; (b) at individual size×colour level, demand is intermittent (ones and twos), where ordinary forecasting breaks and error commonly runs up to ~40%.

Source: Impact Analytics size curves; o9; Style Arcade
Honest data requirement — read before promising ML Statistical forecasting wants ≥18–24 months of history; ML's "20–40% better accuracy" carries the qualifier "for retailers with sufficient data volume." The literature is blunt that fed thin or dirty data, heavy ML loses to simple methods — data quality contributes ~half the performance variation, and "garbage in, garbage out" propagates unchecked through legacy retail data. 78% of companies still forecast in Excel. For ML forecasting to beat a rule, KDPS would need ~2–3 years of clean, validated, size-level sales with stockout/lost-sales correction and consistent attribute tags — exactly what a per-store-POS + Tally + Excel shop doesn't have. Realistic near-term win: attribute tagging + size curves + simple methods, with accuracy expectations set honestly (fashion ~50–65%; new product ~40–55%) and measured in WAPE, not MAPE (MAPE blows up on near-zero actuals). SR Analytics; ToolsGroup; onepint.ai; Trax

5 · "An AI agent that recommends offers" — hype check

Verdict The mature, deployed technology is classic ML / operations-research optimization with a dashboard and a human approval step. "An AI agent that autonomously sets your markdowns" is, as of mid-2026, overwhelmingly marketing wrapped around that same engine — not a separate capability you can buy and trust to run unattended.

What's actually shipping vs branded

  • The numeric optimization is decades-old ML/OR — estimate elasticity, solve a constrained problem ("max margin s.t. sell-through target") inside business rules. Elasticity as a concept dates to 1890; ML demand forecasting has been productized ~15–20 years. Not generative AI. Price optimization
  • "Agentic" pricing is at alpha/pilot. Revionics' multi-agent pricing system — among the most-touted — was an alpha (Apr 2025), pilots in 2025, GA only planned 2026; and it's human-directed (a manager types "change these prices by 10% while holding 2% margin" and the agents execute that request). That's workflow automation under explicit human parameters, not autonomous strategy. Revionics
  • Gartner calls it "agent-washing": only ~130 of thousands of "agentic" vendors are genuinely agentic; >40% of agentic AI projects predicted cancelled by 2027; most current projects are "early-stage experiments… driven by hype and often misapplied." Gartner
  • The clearest tell — Peak: markets "Agentic Commercial Pricing" loudly, but its actual markdown product is ML + guardrails where merchants pick from ranked scenarios — in their own words "AI-recommended, human-approved — not autonomous agents setting prices independently." Peak

What LLMs are genuinely good for here

LLMs are a language layer, not a pricing engine. Three real jobs:

  1. Conversational analytics — "chat with your data" to find trends (e.g. Revionics' shipping Gemini-based feature). The vendor is careful: the LLM helps you understand data; the optimization science still decides. Revionics
  2. Plain-language explanations of why a markdown is recommended — high value because "explainable recommendations beat marginally-more-optimal ones when a human has to approve the price." Umbrex playbook
  3. What-if / scenario summaries and drafting the rationale.
What LLMs must NOT do Compute the number. LLMs "are not calculators" — they fragment numbers when tokenizing and "degrade sharply as constraints are introduced," exactly the regime of markdown optimization. The engineering consensus is a hybrid: LLM orchestrates and explains, deterministic code computes. VentureBeat; Moveo.ai

Vendor announcements assessed: Revionics (most credible GenAI story, but bounded), Blue Yonder ("five agents" are mostly fulfillment/service, not markdown; markdown stays ML), Oracle (agents are horizontal Fusion; retail markdown = LPO optimizer + approval), Impact Analytics ("Agentic AI" branded as a separate offering — telling you the pricing product isn't itself the agent), Peak (loudest gap). Skeptical commentary: PROS on agent-washing.

6 · Human-in-the-loop — recommend, don't auto-apply

This matches your hard rule (AI only suggests; a human authors and approves the price). It's also the industry norm — even tools that can auto-execute are sold as recommenders with explicit approval gates.

The reference workflow — Oracle LPO's named statuses

Optimization runs → recommendations land in Ready for Review → a pricing analyst reviews at item level and may accept, reject, or override any one → status becomes Reviewed → a buyer submits/approves → it goes live. Granular accept/reject/override, plus a separate sign-off.

Oracle workflow docs

Recurring patterns to copy:

  • Accept/reject to auto-create the campaign; manually add/remove specific items (tweak). RELEX
  • Exception-based management — the dashboard shows "exceptions, triggers, recommended actions, expected impact, owners, deadlines"; humans act on flags, not every line. Umbrex
  • Scenario simulation, human picks — options ranked "most profitable → most aggressive." Peak
  • Real reasons humans override — confidence in a product, a regional team wanting deeper cuts. Design for it, and log it. Oracle
Governance guardrails the optimizer works inside (not above) Decision rights + approval thresholds + escalation paths + weekly reviews; and hard rule guardrails: minimum-margin / price floors, psychological price-ending rules, allowed discount ladders, full-price windows & markdown timing, sell-through targets / markdown budget, and per-brand brand-guidelines. All of these map onto KDPS's per-brand SOR/Outright/EOSS and offer rules. Umbrex; Peak; Oracle LPO

7 · Cold-start / small-retailer reality — what one developer actually builds

The unanimous finding A simple, explainable, rules-based markdown system — age buckets + a sell-through trigger + a margin floor — is not a compromise. It's the recommended starting point even by the vendors who sell ML. ML markdown earns its keep only at scale (thousands of SKUs × hundreds of stores) on years of clean history KDPS doesn't have. Heuristics get a small retailer most of the way. Build the rule engine; capture data so ML is a later upgrade, not a rewrite.

Rules-based vs ML — and when rules are "good enough"

Two timing philosophies: calendar/rules-based (predetermined cuts on dates or triggers) vs optimization-based (continuous adjustment against a forecast + elasticity). ML's payoff is a function of scale, not sophistication: a tiny per-item gain only matters "across thousands of SKUs and hundreds of locations." Crucially, a pricing-software vendor (Cognira) says the quiet part out loud: "The core discipline — pre-defined triggers, tiered discounts, a margin floor, post-season analysis — runs fine on a spreadsheet with a weekly review."

Honest limits of rules to design around: a fixed schedule "applies discounts regardless of actual performance" (over-cuts winners, under-cuts duds) and can't differentiate by elasticity. And both rules and ML die on bad data — "if store inventory accuracy is off, markdown decisions get noisy fast." Fix per-store, per-SKU stock accuracy first. Academic backing: heuristic pricing rules are appropriate exactly "if a decision maker wants to set the price of a new product or one whose past price variation is low, and budget limitations prevent experiments" — the cold-start case — and stay "easily understandable and therefore implementable."

Priceva; Cognira; Increff; Review of Managerial Science

Heuristic A — age-bucket → discount ladder (the classic automatic markdown)

Decades of precedent: Filene's Basement (1982) tagged each item with its first-sale date and auto-cut on a clock — day 12: −25%, day 18: −50%, day 24: −75%, day 30: donate. Notably 90% sold in the first 12-day full-price window; the ladder only cleared the tail. Shopify's modern bucket→action version maps nicely onto KDPS's Season/Collection/Age tags: 0–30 days no markdown (try placement/bundling/rebalancing first), 31–90 days planned markdowns, 90+ days liquidate; 180 days = dead stock.

Filene's Basement; Shopify inventory aging; Priceva

Heuristic B — sell-through-triggered markdown (the smarter, still-simple upgrade)

Pure age ladders ignore how much sold. The recommended improvement — same build complexity, far better — is to trigger on sell-through vs target for the weeks elapsed: "Set a sell-through target for each style at each checkpoint. At the midpoint of the selling window, pull actual sell-through. Any style materially below its curve gets a small, automatic markdown that week." Concrete thresholds seen: "below 40% sell-through at week 8 → first markdown" (Cognira); "end-of-season below 60% → markdown trigger" (Eightx/Toolio).

Step sizes and cadence (consistent across sources): first cut shallow 10–25%, only 2–3 steps total (≈15–25% → 30–40% → 50–60%+), re-check 2–3 weeks after each. The margin math to put in the "reason" string — on a ₹100 item costing ₹45: a 15% cut keeps ₹40 gross profit; 30% drops it to ₹25; waiting for 55%-off yields zero. "Small and on time beats deep and late," and it protects brand positioning; predictable deep promos "train customers to wait."

Eightx; Cognira; Toolio

The crawl-walk-run upgrade path

This is an explicit framework from an optimization vendor (Revionics) that endorses starting on rules: Crawl = manage manually / by category in Excel, define price rules. Walk = rules-based management + forecasting, model strategies side-by-side, enforce constraints (margin floors, increments). Run = AI/elasticity optimization, learning per-SKU price sensitivity. The key reassurance: rules don't disappear when ML arrives — they wrap it. Even mature optimization "combines model outputs with business rules" (margin thresholds, increments, blackout periods). So the rule engine you build now is permanent infrastructure.

Revionics crawl-walk-run; McKinsey

Why ML is out of reach now (and heuristics still capture the big win)

ML elasticity models "utilize massive volumes of data" — a McKinsey engine drew on ~10 terabytes of transaction records with per-product features (own price, substitute price, promos, inventory, seasonality, competitor volumes). Elasticity specifically needs observed price variation per item; with little markdown history you can't estimate it — precisely when the literature says use heuristics. The honest framing: ML chases the last increment of margin once you have scale + clean history; the heuristic captures the first, larger increment by simply not marking down too late or too deep.

Price optimization; McKinsey; Review of Managerial Science

Suggested V1 for KDPS (a realistic first version)

  1. V1 = sell-through-triggered ladder, not a pure age ladder. Age alone over/under-cuts; sell-through-vs-target is the same build cost and far better. Use age only as a fallback trigger when a SKU has too little sales data yet.
  2. Per-brand parameters, not global constants. KDPS is multi-brand with brand-specific velocity, return windows, SOR/Outright/EOSS and offer rules — make targets, bucket widths and step sizes per-brand inputs.
  3. Defaults to ship with: a per-brand sell-through target curve (anchor ~65–85% end-of-season); first markdown 15–20% when below curve at mid-season; 2–3 steps (≈15–20% → 30–40% → 50–60%+); re-check every 2–3 weeks; a hard margin floor from PT/invoice cost that a recommendation can never breach.
  4. Treat broken size runs as a first-class trigger (you already store Style×Size×Color) — flag a lot for markdown once the sellable sizes are gone even if overall sell-through looks fine.
  5. Always emit a plain-language reason (sell-through %, week N vs target, resulting band, margin headroom). Explainability is the feature, not decoration — owners on WhatsApp will trust "22% sold by week 6, target 40% → suggest 20% off, still ₹X above cost" over a black-box number. This is the one place an LLM can help — phrase the reason — never compute the number.
  6. Human authors and approves — copy the Oracle pattern: recommend → human accept/reject/override per lot → approve → the price change becomes an authored document. Log overrides.
  7. Capture now the data ML will later need: per-SKU stock-in date, every price change with date, units sold per week per store. With 1–2 clean seasons logged, the "run" (elasticity) stage becomes possible without re-instrumenting.
  8. Don't build optimization yet. At 50 stores you're below the scale where item×store×week optimization pays back, and you lack the price-variation history to estimate elasticity. The rule engine is permanent — ML, when it comes, wraps it.
Fits the architecture rules This matches the master rules cleanly: AI at the edges only (suggest, not decide), flag-don't-block, documents-write-ledgers (the approved markdown is a human-authored price-change document), profitability derived (margin floor from PT cost, not hand-entered). The recommender reads ledgers/snapshots and proposes; it never writes a price.
