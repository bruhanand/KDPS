The two spines of KDPS

Learn just two ideas — one business, one technical — and most of the jargon in your design suddenly clicks into place. One decides the rules (who owns the stock — now four models). The other keeps the data honest.

Why this lesson: you're the architect. These are the two calls that everything else in KDPS inherits — so they're the two you most need in your own words.

1 The business spine

🟧 Who owns the stock? — four models, still one question

Before any software question, one fact decides a brand's entire rulebook: does KDPS own the stock, or does the brand? That question hasn't changed — but each answer now comes in two flavours. So there are four commercial models in all. Set the model on the brand, and the rest follows.

KDPS owns it  → earns a margin

Buy & Sell Outright

Bought outright. No returns — it's yours to clear.

Correction 25-18-10

You own it, but the brand grants a capped return allowance (≈10% of goods, stretchable). Return manually — within the window and within the budget.

Brand owns it  → earns a commission

SOR Sale or Return

Brand owns it; you sell on its behalf. All unsold goes back, no cap. To get more, raise a fresh booking.

Consignment

Same as SOR — but the brand keeps topping up, so no fresh booking each time.

SOR and Consignment are near-identical downstream — the only real difference is the rolling top-up.

See how one fact — the model — pushes outward into everything downstream:

the model→ who owns it→ returns→ margin vs commission→ when you pay the vendor→ which alert fires

💡 Why it's a "spine": set the model once on the brand, and dozens of rules derive from it automatically — returns, how you earn, when you pay the vendor, which alert fires. Get it wrong and it's the most expensive mistake the system prevents: treat a brand-owned line (SOR / Consignment) as KDPS-owned and you'd book a liability that shouldn't exist; miss a Correction return window — or blow its capped allowance — and unreturnable stock becomes dead stock KDPS now owns.

2 The technical spine

🟦 How do you store stock? — Ledger vs Count

Now the data question. There are two ways to remember how much stock you have. One is fragile. One is bulletproof.

❌ A count (fragile)

  • Store one number: "size 8 = 3"
  • Every sale overwrites it
  • If it goes wrong, you can't tell why
  • No history, no audit

✅ A ledger (robust)

  • Store the list of movements
  • Nothing is ever edited — only added
  • The count is added up, not stored
  • Every unit traces to a document

Here's the same SKU as a ledger. The balance on the right is calculated, never typed:

# Nike Air Max 90 · White · Size 8
+5 GRN #41 (received from brand) → 5
−1 Bill #902 (counter sale) → 4
−2 Transfer (to Store 7) → 2
+1 Return (customer exchange) → 3
────────────────────────────────────────
Stock on hand = sum of the column = 3

💡 Why it's a "spine": this is just double-entry accounting applied to stock. It gives you three things for free — a full audit trail, self-healing numbers (your "sell-before-inward / negative SOH" quirk just works), and it scales to millions of rows because each entry is tiny and never changes.

3 The bridge between them

Where AI is allowed to stand

The rules live in the business spine. The truth lives in the technical spine. AI is never allowed to write to either directly.

🤖 AI proposes. The spine records. A rule or a human sits in between.
The invoice-reader, the PT-mapper, the demand forecast — each one only ever makes a draft with a confidence score. A rule auto-accepts it, or a person taps approve. If the AI is wrong, you've lost a draft — not corrupted your stock or your money.

That's the whole shape of KDPS: a boring, bulletproof spine in the middle, a ring of clever, fallible AI on the edge, and rules + humans guarding every door between them.
